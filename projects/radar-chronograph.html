24 GHz Doppler Radar Chronograph

A self-directed RF engineering project: a 24 GHz Doppler radar built to measure projectile velocity, using integrated patch antenna arrays in place of external RF connectors.

Band: 24.125 GHz Architecture: FMCW Doppler radar MCU: ESP32-S3 Tools: KiCad · OpenEMS · FreeCAD Status: Antenna validation in progress

Background

While deployed, I saw a Garmin Xero being used to measure muzzle velocity and thought the concept was neat. I have a background in electrical engineering, so I decided to see whether I could design a Doppler radar chronograph of my own — out of personal curiosity, and as a way to challenge my RF engineering skills and push them into a higher-frequency range than I typically work in. RF hardware design of this kind isn't something I do in my day job; this project is entirely a personal build.

The design has gone through two major antenna generations. The first used hand-wound helical antenna elements, deliberately placed on their own board separate from the transceiver electronics so antenna designs could be swapped without touching the rest of the system. The current generation replaces that entirely with integrated microstrip patch antenna arrays etched directly onto the PCB — the version documented in detail below.

Why Doppler Radar, and What I'm Actually Measuring

Measuring a bullet's velocity is a much narrower problem than radar design in general. There's no need to track a target or resolve range — the only thing that matters is extracting a speed value from a frequency shift. That keeps the architecture to a bare-bones Doppler radar rather than the more complex ranging or tracking systems used elsewhere.

The intended target is the base of a 5.56×45mm NATO round, viewed at very short range (1.5–2 ft) as it exits the muzzle. The base is the most favorable aspect available for radar reflection, so I worked out the expected radar cross-section (RCS) treating it as a flat circular plate viewed head-on:

σ = 4π A² / λ²

A = π r²  with r = 2.85 mm  →  A = 25.5 mm²
λ = 12.43 mm at 24.125 GHz

σ ≈ 5.24 × 10⁻⁵ m² = −32.8 dBm2 (flat base, idealized)
dBm2 = dB per squar meter

Most 5.56 ammunition has a boat-tail base rather than a flat one, which drops the RCS further — I used a conservative design value of −38 dBm2 to make sure the link budget wasn't flattered by an overly optimistic target. For reference, a nose-on aspect would be roughly −42 dBm2, so working the base aspect is worth 4–9 dB over other viewing angles, which is part of why the sensor geometry is arranged the way it is.

System Architecture

The RF chain is built around Analog Devices' 24 GHz ISM chipset: an ADF5901 transmitter generating the FMCW chirp, and an ADF5904 four-channel downconverter on receive. A MAX11043 16-bit sigma-delta ADC digitizes the downconverted signal, and an ESP32-S3 handles system control, FFT processing, and connectivity.

Only one transmit output and one receive channel are populated. With the SNR margin this system has at short range, there's no need for MIMO or angle-of-arrival estimation — the event being detected is essentially binary (a projectile crossed the beam), plus a velocity from the Doppler shift. The tradeoff is that the system can't tell where in the beam a projectile passed, only that it did; adding that later would mean a new receive antenna topology, not just a firmware change.

From External Connectors to Integrated Patch Arrays

The earlier board revision used six external SMPM connectors so antennas could be swapped and the RF chain could be bench-tested independently of the radiating elements. The current revision replaces all six with two integrated 4×4 microstrip patch arrays — one for transmit, one for receive — etched directly onto the board.

That's a real tradeoff, not a free upgrade. Integrating the antennas removes the RF connector cost entirely and roughly $130 in external antenna and connector cost against a modest increase in PCB cost and board area, but it also means giving up the ability to characterize the RF chain independently of the antenna, and giving up the ability to swap antenna designs without a new board spin. I considered mitigations — 0Ω jumpers, a dual-populate variant — and decided the simpler fully-integrated design was worth the tradeoff for this build.

Array sizing came directly from the beamwidth I was targeting. A rough beamwidth-to-array-size relationship (θ ≈ 51°/(N·d/λ) at half-wavelength spacing) called for about 3.4 elements per side for a 30° beam, which I rounded up to 4×4 for symmetry — giving a predicted gain around 14.6 dBi per array.

Link Budget: Getting It to Close

The link budget went through several iterations as the design firmed up, and the history is worth keeping because it shows exactly which assumptions the design is leaning on. The first pass — FR4 substrate, a smaller 2×2/4×1 array configuration, 5 ft range — didn't close; the return signal landed below the receiver's noise floor. Two changes rescued it: pulling the range in to 1.5–2 ft (range appears to the fourth power in the radar equation, so this alone was worth about 14 dB) and moving to the larger 4×4 arrays for more gain.

That processing gain is genuinely available, not just a number on paper: a bullet at ~3,000 fps crosses a 2 ft beam in about 0.2 ms, and the ADF5901 supports chirp times as short as 10 μs — enough for roughly 20 usable chirps during transit, of which 16-chirp coherent integration accounts for the +12 dB above. Even after derating for manufacturing variation, temperature drift, and off-normal bullet yaw (about 8 dB combined), the design still closes with roughly 20 dB of margin.

Open item: this budget assumes FR4-level antenna losses as a conservative placeholder. The current stackup actually puts the radiating layer on a low-loss high-frequency laminate (below), which should recover several dB — the budget hasn't been re-run against the as-built substrate yet.

PCB Stackup

The board uses a hybrid stackup: ordinary FR4 for the inner digital and power layers, and a high-frequency laminate only for the bottom dielectric where the antennas actually radiate. That keeps the cost of the bulk of the board low while giving the radiating layer the low-loss, controlled-permittivity substrate that a 24 GHz patch antenna needs.

This is a deliberate change from an earlier all-FR4 stackup, whose radiating dielectric was far too thin to support an efficient patch at this frequency. The 50 Ω microstrip width on this stackup works out to about 0.55 mm, and the ground plane under the antenna area has to stay completely solid — no vias, no trace crossings, no plane splits — or the antennas detune.

Antenna Design — and an Open Problem

Patch dimensions for εr = 3.48 at 24 GHz work out analytically to roughly 4.2 mm × 3.3 mm using the standard transmission-line patch model. That's what's currently drawn in the PCB footprints. But when I ran a single-element simulation in OpenEMS against that exact geometry, it resonated at 19.1 GHz — about 5 GHz low — which would imply the patches are oversized by roughly 26%.

I'm treating that result as unconfirmed rather than as a correction, for a specific reason: the same simulation also returned a return loss of essentially 0 dB at its own resonant frequency, meaning the model shows the antenna accepting almost no power at any frequency. That's the signature of a feed or port modeling problem, not a real electromagnetic result — a correctly modeled patch that's simply mis-sized should still show real power acceptance at its (wrong) resonance. So right now I have one analytic method and one simulation disagreeing by 26%, and I don't yet trust the simulation enough to redraw the footprints on its say-so.

Current plan to close this: cross-check the analytic dimensions with a second independent method (cavity model), then re-run the simulation in FreeCAD against the actual exported PCB geometry — including the real feed network — rather than an idealized standalone patch, since the port/feed model is the most likely source of the discrepancy. If those still disagree, a cheap FR4 test coupon with a small sweep of patch sizes, measured on a VNA, settles it empirically.

Simulation Notes: a Useful FDTD Lesson

Worth recording since it cost real time to learn: early OpenEMS runs wouldn't converge at all. The cause turned out to be a too-narrow simulation bandwidth — in FDTD, the excitation pulse length is inversely proportional to the bandwidth you request, so asking for a narrow 250 MHz window implied an extremely long pulse and correspondingly enormous run times. Widening the simulated bandwidth to several GHz around the center frequency shortened the pulse dramatically and converged quickly, with no loss of accuracy at the actual frequency of interest. It's a good example of a simulation-tool quirk that has nothing to do with the physics of the antenna itself, but will absolutely stop you cold if you don't know to look for it.

Current Status

The schematic work — replacing six external SMPM connectors with the two integrated patch arrays — is complete and committed. The PCB has the arrays placed and old traces cleared, but RF routing hasn't started, and fabrication is intentionally blocked until the antenna dimension question above is resolved. Ordering a board before that's settled would risk spending a high-frequency-laminate fabrication run on an antenna that doesn't work at the design frequency.

Takeaways

  • Reducing the problem to what's actually required (speed only, no tracking or ranging) kept a genuinely complex sensor class tractable for a personal project
  • Range dominates a short-range radar link budget more than almost any other variable, thanks to the fourth-power falloff — a 3× reduction in range bought more margin than any antenna or power change could
  • Working the most favorable target aspect (the bullet base, not a broadside or nose-on view) was worth several dB before any hardware decision was made
  • A simulation result that contradicts a well-established analytic model is a reason to check the simulation's own internal consistency before trusting it over the analysis
  • Knowing when not to commit to fabrication — when a real open question could waste an expensive board run — is as much a part of the engineering as the design work itself
